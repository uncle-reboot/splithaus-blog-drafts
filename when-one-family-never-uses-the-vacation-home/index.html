When one family never uses the vacation home

By Dan·Publishes on launch day

Part of Splitting the calendar. Start with How to share a vacation home with family: five ways to split the calendar.

Short answer

When one household rarely uses a shared vacation home, start by asking why instead of assuming. Then agree what happens to time nobody uses: a release date that opens it to everyone, weeknights open all year, or letting a group offer its nights to another. Whether time follows ownership or use, and who pays what, are separate decisions to settle at the yearly meeting.

Sometimes one household in a shared vacation home uses the house much less than the others. Sometimes it is a little less. Sometimes a branch of the family has not been in years. Their weeks sit on the calendar, the house sits empty, and the rest of the family starts to wonder whether they can use it.

This guide covers how to raise it, the common ways to let unused time reach someone who wants it, and the bigger questions it can lead to.

Start by asking why

It is tempting to assume that a family that never comes does not care about the house. The reason may be something else:

  • Distance. They moved across the country, and the trip costs a day each way.
  • School and work. Their children's schedules or their jobs only allow the busiest weeks, which someone else always has.
  • Health or age. Travel, stairs, or the drive have become hard.
  • Money. The trip costs more than they can spend right now.
  • Feelings about the house. Memories, or an old disagreement, keep them away.

Each reason points to a different answer. A family that only has school holidays free needs a different fix than a family that has stopped wanting to go. Ask, and listen before proposing anything.

Three ways unused time can reach someone else

Families use one or more of these. The Shared House Playbook asks about all three.

A release date

If a group has not booked its time a set number of days before it starts, anyone can book it. For example: Group 1 cannot use July 22 to 26. Fourteen days before, nobody from Group 1 has booked it, so it opens to everyone, and Group 2 books it.

  • Good for: keeping each group's time its own until close to the date, while making sure the house does not sit empty by default.
  • Agree: how many days before, and whether the group gives notice or the date passes on its own.

Weeknights open to everyone

Monday to Thursday nights are open to anyone, all year, outside the busiest weeks. Each group keeps its weekends and its turns.

  • Good for: families with members who live close enough for short visits.
  • Agree: which weeks are excluded, such as the summer peak and the holidays.

Offering time to another group

A group that cannot use its time offers those nights to another group of its choosing. The group keeps control of who gets its time.

  • Good for: households who will not use the house this year but want a say in who does.
  • Agree: whether offered time counts against the receiving group's share.

These work together. For example, a release date can be the default, and a group can offer its time sooner if it knows early.

Bring it up at the yearly meeting

Unused time is simpler to talk about at a regular meeting, when it is about the rules rather than about one person. Our guide to inheriting a vacation home with siblings suggests a short yearly meeting for this kind of question. A useful way to put it: "Some weeks went unused this year. Do we want a rule for that?"

The bigger questions it can lead to

Sometimes unused time is a sign of something larger. Two questions often follow:

Should time follow ownership or use? Owning, using, and paying are three different things. A group that owns a third of the house but comes once a year may still want its full share of time in the written plan. Agree whether time stays the same for every group, follows ownership, or follows how much each household actually uses the house.

Who pays what? Some families keep costs split by ownership no matter who uses the house. Others add a nightly fee for using it. This is a separate decision from the calendar. Agree on it at the same time and keep the two decisions separate.

If a household no longer wants a share in the house at all, that is a different conversation, about ownership rather than scheduling. It depends on how the house is owned and on local law, so take it to a lawyer or tax advisor.

Put it in writing

Whatever you agree, write it down where every group can see it: the release rule, which nights are open, and how offering time works. Our guide to five ways to split the calendar covers how these rules fit with the rest of your calendar. The free Shared House Playbook asks your family what happens to nights nobody uses and puts the answer in a one-page house plan.

Frequently asked questions

Can we use a sibling's weeks if they never come? Only under a rule everyone has agreed to, such as a release date. Using someone's time without one tends to cause bigger problems than the empty week.

How many days before should unused time open up? Pick a number that fits how far ahead your family plans trips. A shorter window keeps each group's time its own for longer. A longer one gives others time to plan.

Should a family that never comes still pay its share? That depends on how your family has decided to split costs. Some split by ownership regardless of use, and some add a nightly fee for using the house. Decide it as a family and write it down.
