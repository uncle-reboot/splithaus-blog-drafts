Who gets the Fourth of July? A holiday rotation for a shared vacation home

By Dan·Publishes on launch day

Part of Splitting the calendar. Start with How to share a vacation home with family: five ways to split the calendar.

Short answer

There are four common ways to handle holidays at a shared house: treat them like any other day, rotate each holiday to the next group every year, count holidays for more of a group's share, or keep them for everyone. A simple rotation, written down, lets every group see years ahead whose turn it is.

At a shared vacation home, the dates most likely to need a plan are the holidays: the Fourth of July, Labor Day, Thanksgiving, and the week between Christmas and New Year. Ordinary weeks can follow your usual rules. Holidays need their own.

This guide covers four ways families handle holidays, and gives rotation examples for three and four groups that you can copy and change.

First, decide which holidays count

Before choosing a method, agree on the list. A holiday that nobody in the family cares about does not need a rule, and a missing one will cause trouble later. For each holiday, also agree exactly which days it covers:

  • The Fourth of July: the Saturday-to-Saturday week that includes July 4 is a common choice. When July 4 falls on a weekend, decide whether it belongs to the week before or the week after.
  • Long weekends: Memorial Day and Labor Day are usually Friday to Monday.
  • Thanksgiving: Wednesday to Sunday, or the full week if school is out.
  • Christmas and New Year: one week or two, and whether Christmas and New Year count as one holiday or two.

Write the dates down. "Thanksgiving" means different things to different households.

Four ways to handle holidays

1. Treat holidays like any other day

Holidays go to whoever's turn it is, under your usual calendar rules.

  • Good for: families who do not have strong feelings about any one date.
  • Watch for: if your rotation is fixed, the same group can end up with the same holiday every year.

2. Rotate each holiday to the next group every year

Each holiday has its own order, and the order moves by one group each year. Over a few years, every group has every holiday once.

  • Good for: families who want every group to get every holiday over time. It is simple to explain.
  • Watch for: with more holidays than groups, or more groups than holidays, some years are uneven. The examples below show how to handle that.

3. Count holidays for more of a group's share

A holiday week counts as more than a normal week. For example, the Fourth of July week might count as one and a half weeks of a group's summer share.

  • Good for: families who split time by a set number of weeks and want the most wanted weeks to cost more.
  • Watch for: the extra arithmetic. Agree on the numbers in advance and keep them simple.

4. Keep holidays for everyone

Nobody owns the holiday. The whole family is welcome, and the house is shared that week.

  • Good for: families who see the holidays as the time everyone gets together.
  • Watch for: the house has a limit on how many people can stay. Agree what happens when everyone wants to come.

A rotation for three groups

This example is hypothetical. Three groups, A, B, and C, share three summer holidays. Each year, every holiday moves to the next group.

Memorial Day weekend Fourth of July week Labor Day weekend
Year 1 A B C
Year 2 B C A
Year 3 C A B
Year 4 A B C

Every group has every holiday once in three years, and each group has exactly one summer holiday every year. In year 4 the pattern starts again.

A rotation for four groups

This example is also hypothetical. Four groups, A, B, C, and D, share four holidays across the year.

Fourth of July week Labor Day weekend Thanksgiving Christmas week
Year 1 A B C D
Year 2 B C D A
Year 3 C D A B
Year 4 D A B C

Every group has one holiday a year, and every holiday once in four years.

When the numbers do not match

The tables above work because the number of holidays matches the number of groups. When it does not, here are three ways to handle it:

  • Fewer holidays than groups. Some groups have no holiday that year. The rotation still moves by one, so no group goes without for long. Write the order down so nobody has to remember whose turn was skipped.
  • More holidays than groups. Some groups get two holidays in a year. Pair a big holiday with a smaller one so the pairs feel even.
  • Different group sizes. If one group has much more time than the others, decide whether holidays follow the same split or rotate evenly. Either can work. Agree on it before the first year.

When a group cannot use its holiday

Plans change. Decide in advance what happens to a holiday a group cannot use:

  • It passes to the next group in the order for that year only, and the rotation carries on as normal next year.
  • It opens to everyone after a set date, the same as any other unused night. Our guide to when one family never uses the house covers release dates in more detail.
  • The group can offer it to another group of its choosing.

The important part is that giving up a holiday one year does not change the rotation for the years after.

Put it in writing

A holiday rotation only works if everyone can look it up. Write the list of holidays, the exact dates each one covers, and the order for the next few years in one place every group can see. It saves the family from reconstructing whose turn it was from old messages.

Frequently asked questions

What is a fair way to share the Fourth of July at a family vacation home? A rotation that moves the holiday to the next group every year is a simple answer, because over a few years every group gets it the same number of times. Fair here means a rule everyone agreed to and that is applied the same way every year.

Should holidays count for more than other weeks? Some families count a holiday week as more than a normal week so the most wanted dates cost more of a group's share. It works well if the numbers are simple and agreed in advance.

What if two groups want to spend the holiday together? Let them. A group can always invite another group into its time, as long as the house is not over its limit on how many people can stay.
