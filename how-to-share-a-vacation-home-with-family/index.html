How to share a vacation home with family: five ways to split the calendar

By Matt Wells·Draft, not yet published

Short answer

Most families split a shared vacation home in one of five ways: fixed weeks, a rotation, a draft, booking as you go with limits, or matching everyone's preferences. Decide which one fits your group, how holidays work, and what happens to nights nobody uses. Then write it down where everyone can see it.

Sharing a vacation home with family usually starts simply. Everyone knows when they like to go, and someone keeps track. Then the family grows, a second and third household want the same summer weeks, and the question of who gets which dates gets harder to settle by phone.

There is no single right way to divide the calendar. There are a handful of common ways, and each one suits a different kind of group. This guide walks through five of them, the questions every family has to answer whichever one it picks, and a worked example.

First, agree on who is sharing

Before you talk about dates, agree on who the calendar is divided between. Most families share by group: one group per sibling, per branch of the family, or per household. Individual people book inside their group's time.

Getting this right first makes everything else easier. "Three groups get equal time" is a clear starting point. "Everyone gets a fair amount" is not.

The five ways to split the calendar

1. Fixed weeks

Each group gets the same weeks every year. The Smiths always have the second week of July, and the Joneses always have the last week of August.

  • Good for: families who like predictability and plan around school calendars and work leave.
  • Watch for: the best weeks never move. A group that is stuck with early June every year may stop feeling like the arrangement is shared.

2. Take turns (a rotation)

Each group gets a block of time in a repeating order, and the order shifts each year. If Group 1 had the first July week this year, Group 2 has it next year.

  • Good for: spreading the most wanted weeks around over several years.
  • Watch for: a rotation needs a written record of whose turn is next. Memory is not a record.

3. Draft your dates

Groups take turns picking the nights or weeks they want, one pick at a time, in an agreed order. The order usually reverses or rotates each round so the group that picks last in one round picks first in the next.

  • Good for: groups whose plans change from year to year, and who want a say in their dates rather than a fixed slot.
  • Watch for: a draft takes coordination. Everyone has to be available to pick, or you need a rule for what happens when someone does not.

4. Book as you go, with limits

Anyone can book open nights, first come first served, with limits that leave room for everyone. Common limits are a maximum number of nights per booking, a maximum number of peak weeks per group, or a cap on how far ahead someone can book.

  • Good for: smaller groups, houses that are often empty, and families who decide trips at short notice.
  • Watch for: without limits, the most organized person tends to get the best dates.

5. Match everyone's preferences

Each group rates the dates it wants, and the schedule is built by balancing everyone's shares, preferences, and flexibility. Some families do this by hand at a meeting. Tools such as SplitHaus Match do it from the ratings members enter.

  • Good for: groups where several households want overlapping dates and want to see their preferences taken into account.
  • Watch for: everyone needs to rate their dates before the schedule is built, and the group still decides the rules the schedule follows.

Many families mix these. A common combination is a rotation for summer weeks and booking as you go for the rest of the year.

Three questions every family has to answer

Whichever approach you pick, the same three questions come up. Answer them early.

How long is a turn or a pick, and what day does it start? A week that runs Saturday to Saturday is easy to plan around. Mixing Saturday and Sunday starts causes overlaps.

How do holidays work? The Fourth of July, Thanksgiving, and the week between Christmas and New Year are usually the most wanted dates. Families tend to choose one of four answers: holidays are treated like any other day, each holiday passes to the next group every year, holidays count for more of a group's share, or holidays are for everyone.

What happens to nights nobody uses? Plans change. Agree how an open night reaches someone who wants it. A simple rule is a release date: if a group has not booked its time a set number of days before, anyone can book it. Some families also keep weeknights open to everyone all year.

A worked example

This example is hypothetical. It shows how one set of answers fits together.

Three sibling groups share a lake house. They want the twelve summer weeks split evenly and the holidays to move around.

  • Approach: take turns. Each group gets four one-week blocks, Saturday to Saturday, in a repeating order. The order shifts by one group each year.
  • Holidays: the Fourth of July week passes to the next group every year. It counts as one of that group's four weeks.
  • Open nights: if a group has not booked its week 14 days before it starts, anyone can book it. Weeknights outside the summer are open to everyone.
  • Record: the order, this year's weeks, and the holiday rotation are written down in one place every group can see.

In year one, Group 1 cannot use a week in late July. Fourteen days before, nobody from Group 1 has booked it, so it opens up, and Group 2 books it. Nobody had to negotiate. The rule was agreed in advance.

Write it down

The approach matters less than having one that everyone has agreed to and can look up. A written plan answers "whose turn is it?" without anyone having to remember.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest way to share a vacation home with family? For two or three groups, a rotation of equal blocks with a release date for unused time is usually the simplest to explain and keep track of.

How do you split summer weeks fairly? Agree on the share each group gets first, then pick a method that moves the most wanted weeks around over time, such as a rotation or a draft with a rotating pick order. Fairness here means a rule everyone agreed to, applied the same way every year.

Should the person who uses the house most pay more? Some families split costs equally and some add a nightly fee for using the house. That is a separate decision from the calendar. Agree on it at the same time so the two do not get tangled later.
