The calendar clauses of a shared vacation home agreement

Proposed byline: Dan, subject to his approval. Proposed Friday: October 30, 2026; release hour pending.

Short answer

Write down who shares the calendar, how dates are allocated, how holidays work, when unused nights become available, and how changes are confirmed. Name the current calendar and the person who keeps it up to date. This is an everyday house plan, not an ownership agreement or legal document.

A shared vacation home agreement needs more than a list of who gets which week. It also needs answers for a late cancellation, a holiday that crosses two turns, and a change nobody has put on the calendar yet.

The calendar part can be short. What matters is that each household can read it and know what to do next.

This guide covers scheduling only. It does not provide legal, tax, estate, or ownership advice. Take questions about ownership rights or the effect of proposed rules to a qualified professional. If your house already has legal documents, have any possible conflict with them reviewed before adopting a plan.

Our Shared House Playbook separates time, costs, care, and group decisions. This guide focuses on the time section: the few sentences that connect an agreed method to the dates people actually use.

1. Say who gets a turn

Start with the scheduling groups, not a list of every person who might visit. A group might be one person, a couple, a household, or a family branch that divides its own time.

Write down whether a grown child uses a parent's allocation or has a separate turn. Do not quietly add a new calendar group because someone asks to book independently. If the group wants to change how time is divided, discuss that as a separate decision.

Example wording to adapt:

For scheduling, our groups are _. Each group names one contact. People within a group arrange their use of that group's time together. We discuss adding or changing scheduling groups at _.

A scheduling list does not establish who owns the house, who inherits, or who can make ownership decisions. Keep those questions out of this template.

2. Describe how dates are allocated

“Everyone gets a share” leaves several questions open. Is the share measured in nights or weeks? Are there fixed turns? Can people choose dates? Does unused time count against next year's allocation?

Choose the method before choosing the calendar tool. Our guide to five ways to split the calendar compares fixed weeks, rotations, drafts, booking as you go, and matching preferences. Each needs different instructions.

For a rotation, record the starting order, block length, and changeover day. For a draft, record when choices happen, how many nights each pick covers, and how the picking order changes. For booking as you go, record the booking window and any limits on nights held at once.

Example wording to adapt:

We allocate dates by _. The schedule covers through _. Each group receives . The order starts with _ and changes next year by _. We record the agreed dates before anyone makes travel arrangements.

Leave out rules your group does not use. A short, complete description is more useful than several alternatives left in the finished plan.

3. Give holidays their own rule

A repeating rotation can put the same holiday with the same group more often than intended. Decide whether that is acceptable, whether holidays rotate separately, or whether some dates are shared gatherings.

Name the exact dates or nights covered by a holiday. “Thanksgiving” could mean one night, a long weekend, or a school break. Also say which rule applies when the holiday allocation overlaps an ordinary turn.

Example wording to adapt:

Our separately allocated holidays are _. Each covers . We allocate them by _. For those nights, the holiday schedule takes priority over the ordinary rotation. The ordinary rotation resumes on .

For a worked rotation, see sharing holiday weeks between groups. Use the article to choose an approach, then put only your group's actual rule in the plan.

4. Separate a cancellation from an unbooked night

These are different situations:

  • A group has a confirmed stay and can no longer come.
  • A group has an allocated turn but has not confirmed a stay.
  • A night has not been allocated to anyone.

A release rule should say which situation it covers. “Open two weeks before” is incomplete if nobody knows whether an existing booking can be displaced.

Example wording to adapt:

A group that cannot use a confirmed stay tells _ through . We open unbooked nights to all groups _ days before arrival. This does not cancel a confirmed stay. A new stay is confirmed when . Releasing unused time does not change next year's allocation unless we separately agree otherwise.

The notice period for cancellations and the release window for unbooked nights do not have to be the same. Choose each deliberately. Our guide to unused time at a shared vacation home explores the conversation behind those choices.

5. Write down how a change becomes final

A request in a group chat is not necessarily a confirmed change. Decide who needs to answer and when the shared calendar is updated.

For a swap, distinguish a proposal from both groups accepting. For a released stay, say whether the original group can take it back after someone else has booked. Do not leave either question to whoever happens to be reading the messages first.

Example wording to adapt:

We request changes through _. A change involving another group's confirmed dates needs that group's agreement. A swap is final only when both groups confirm and _ updates the shared calendar. Until then, the existing dates stand. After released nights are confirmed for another group, the original group asks that group before making another change.

This is a proposed process for your group to discuss, not a description of an automatic feature in any app.

6. Name one calendar and its keeper

Record where the current calendar lives, who can edit it, and who steps in when the usual coordinator is away. Keep the plan and calendar linked so someone seeing a date can also find the rules behind it.

If you use Google Calendar, check the permissions rather than assuming every invited person has the same access. Google's sharing instructions distinguish viewing access, event-editing access, and permission to manage sharing. Those settings control what someone can do in the tool; your group still needs to agree what changes they should make.

Example wording to adapt:

Our current calendar is at _. The scheduling coordinator is and the backup is _. Calendar editing access belongs to . Every group checks its dates by _. If a message and the calendar disagree, we ask _ to confirm the intended dates before relying on either.

Do not put access codes, private account credentials, or personal travel details into a publicly shared plan.

Try the wording against a changed plan

Here is a hypothetical example, not a customer story or a recommended universal rule.

Three households rotate seven-night turns. Separately allocated holiday nights override the rotation. Their plan opens unbooked nights to everyone fourteen days before arrival, while protecting confirmed stays. A coordinator records changes.

One household says it cannot use its allocated turn. Another wants just the last three nights. Before anyone books travel, they check:

  1. Is any part of the turn already a confirmed stay?
  2. Do separate holiday nights fall inside it?
  3. Has the release window opened, or does this need an agreed transfer?
  4. Who confirms the three-night stay and records it?
  5. Do the remaining nights stay available under the same rule?

If the written plan cannot answer those questions, revise the wording before treating the calendar entry as final. The point is to test an ordinary change, not to predict every possible disagreement.

Put the decisions on one page

Once you have discussed the options, keep a short record:

  • Groups: who receives time and who answers for each group.
  • Allocation: method, amount of time, order, and scheduling period.
  • Holidays: named nights and which rule takes priority.
  • Unused nights: notice, release window, and protection for confirmed stays.
  • Changes: who agrees, who records them, and when they become final.
  • Current copy: calendar location, coordinator, backup, plan version, and next review date.

Staying at the house is a separate section. Pair the calendar plan with your house rules and checkout checklist, rather than adding cleaning instructions to every booking rule.

At your next house meeting, keep what still fits and discuss what has changed. Record the new version and tell every group where to find it. Do not quietly rewrite a rule while someone is relying on the old one.

Frequently asked questions

Is a calendar plan the same as a legal vacation home agreement?

No. This guide is about everyday scheduling choices. It does not establish ownership, inheritance, sale terms, or legal rights. Take those questions, and any conflict with existing documents, to a qualified professional.

Does every house need a holiday rotation?

No. Some groups keep holidays within ordinary turns; others reserve them for everyone together. Write down the approach your group actually chooses, including the nights it covers.

Can we use a shared calendar without a written plan?

You can record dates that way, but the calendar alone may not explain who can claim an open night or approve a change. Keep the short set of rules somewhere every group can read.

Where should we start?

Open the free Shared House Playbook together. Use its questions to choose the arrangements that fit your house, then keep the written plan beside your calendar. Leave unresolved questions marked for discussion rather than filling them in for everyone else.
